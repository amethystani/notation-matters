\section{Introduction}

The deployment of large language models (LLMs) in computational chemistry has accelerated markedly, with general-purpose and domain-specific models now applied to molecular property prediction, reaction planning, and retrosynthesis.\cite{bran2024,white2023,jablonka2024,jablonka2024predictive} Understanding whether these general-purpose models encode genuine chemical knowledge---rather than surface-level string statistics---is a central question for the themed collection on ``General Purpose Models: LLMs and Beyond.'' A shared but rarely examined assumption across LLM chemistry applications is that models process the \textit{molecule} being described rather than the \textit{notation} used to describe it. Yet the same molecule admits multiple valid representations: SMILES\cite{weininger1988} encodes connectivity and stereochemistry as a linear character string; IUPAC names apply systematic nomenclature rules; InChI\cite{heller2015} provides a canonical layered identifier; and SELFIES\cite{krenn2020} guarantees syntactic validity by construction, eliminating chemically impossible strings. If a model truly internalises molecular concepts, its property predictions should be robust to this choice.

Recent evidence challenges this assumption. Yan \textit{et al.}\cite{yan2025} demonstrated that state-of-the-art LLMs achieve $\leq1\%$ consistency across SMILES and IUPAC representations for water solubility prediction and---crucially---that consistency and accuracy are orthogonal: a more consistent model is not necessarily more accurate. Independently, Ganeeva \textit{et al.}\cite{ganeeva2025} showed that chemistry LMs remain non-robust to SMILES augmentations across regression and captioning tasks. These studies establish notation as a hidden confound that accuracy-focused training cannot resolve. Two critical gaps remain: (a) neither study includes SELFIES, a validity-guaranteed notation whose structural properties may confer robustness advantages; and (b) both are purely behavioural---no study has localised \textit{where in a chemistry encoder} notation-specific information is encoded, which is essential for designing notation-robust architectures.

The second gap is addressable through mechanistic interpretability, a paradigm developed primarily for natural language tasks. Causal tracing has localised factual knowledge in GPT-family models\cite{meng2022} and information-flow analyses have revealed how in-context learning is mediated by attention patterns,\cite{wang2023} yet to my knowledge no analogous mechanistic study exists for chemistry language models. This paper fills both gaps.

I present three contributions. (1) A four-notation behavioural study (SMILES, IUPAC, InChI, SELFIES) of 1072 ESOL molecules across four structural classes, extending the scope and sample size of prior work.\cite{yan2025,ganeeva2025} (2) The identification that SELFIES achieves the lowest sign-flip rate across all four notations (rate 3.2$\times$ lower than SMILES; Fisher exact: OR\,=\,3.34 for SMILES relative to SELFIES, $p\,=\,6.2\!\times\!10^{-6}$), while InChI achieves the lowest MAE, revealing a previously unreported split between catastrophic and average prediction error. (3) To my knowledge, the first mechanistic investigation of cross-notation representation divergence in a chemistry encoder---going beyond purely behavioural characterisation: I show that CLS representations of chemically identical SMILES diverge monotonically with encoder depth, that divergence onset concentrates at $L_2$--$L_3$, and that the magnitude of representational divergence predicts the magnitude of prediction disagreement. Controlled hidden-state replacement further shows that diverged representations retain molecule-specific information without converging on a shared encoding, locating the phenomenon as a failure of convergence rather than a loss of chemical identity. A schematic overview of both study phases is provided in Fig.~\ref{fig:toc}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Behavioural inconsistency study}

\subsubsection{Dataset and notation generation.~~} I applied the behavioural benchmark to all 1128 molecules in the ESOL water solubility dataset.\cite{delaney2004} IUPAC names and InChI identifiers were retrieved via the PubChem REST API compound-name lookup endpoint;\cite{kim2021} 56 lookups failed, leaving 1072 molecules with complete notation sets. SMILES strings were taken directly from ESOL; ground-truth $\logS$ values span $-11.60$ to $+1.58$\,log$_{10}$(mol\,L$^{-1}$). SELFIES were generated using the \texttt{selfies} library (v2.1.1)\cite{krenn2020} by direct SMILES conversion; 958 of 1072 (89.4\%) molecules yielded valid SELFIES representations with successful LLM responses. Molecules were assigned to structural classes by priority-ordered SMILES pattern matching, in the order: heterocyclic ($n=427$; heteroaromatic N or S in ring), aromatic ($n=267$; aromatic ring atoms), carboxylic/ester ($n=51$; C(=O)O sub-structure), halogenated ($n=63$; F, Cl, Br, or I), alcohol/phenol ($n=177$), amine ($n=7$), and aliphatic ($n=80$) as the terminal case. Because assignment is priority-ordered and returns a single label, molecules bearing several functional groups are assigned to whichever rule matches first. This is a property of the labelling scheme rather than a chemical claim, and it applies to most of the dataset: 690 of 1072 molecules (64.4\%) match more than one rule, the commonest co-occurrences being aromatic with halogenated (101 molecules), heterocyclic with aromatic and alcohol/phenol (73), and carboxylic/ester with alcohol/phenol (45). Section~3.3 reports a subgroup analysis testing whether my class-level conclusions depend on this ordering. ESOL records each molecule in a single neutral form, and no tautomer enumeration or protonation-state assignment was performed, so the classification is insensitive to ionisation state by construction.

\subsubsection{LLM querying protocol.~~} I used Qwen3.6:35b-a3b,\cite{qwen2025} a 35\,B-total / 3\,B-active-parameter mixture-of-experts model, run locally via Ollama at temperature\,=\,0 (fully deterministic). Qwen3-series models route chain-of-thought tokens through a dedicated \texttt{think} field in the Ollama API; setting \texttt{think=False} suppresses this extended reasoning output and ensures all returned tokens constitute the final numerical answer. Each molecule was queried independently for each notation with the prompt:

\begin{quote}\small
\textit{``You are a chemistry expert. Predict the water solubility of the molecule below as $\log S$ ($\log_{10}$ molar solubility). Molecule (NOTATION): IDENTIFIER. Reply with ONLY one decimal number between $-10.0$ and $2.0$. No text, no units.''}
\end{quote}

\noindent where NOTATION was replaced by the notation system label (e.g., ``SMILES'', ``IUPAC'', ``InChI'', ``SELFIES'') and IDENTIFIER by the molecule's representation under that system. The notation label was therefore explicit in each query; the observed inconsistency across notations thus reflects the model's differential processing of identical molecules \textit{despite} being informed of the representation type.

\textbf{Decoding and parsing.} Generation used \texttt{temperature}\,=\,0, \texttt{num\_predict}\,=\,12 and \texttt{think}\,=\,\texttt{False}; no other sampling parameters were modified from Ollama defaults, and no system prompt was used. Responses were parsed by extracting the first numeric token matching \texttt{-?\textbackslash d+\textbackslash.?\textbackslash d*} and accepting it only if it fell within $[-10.0, 2.0]$; a response was retried once and otherwise recorded as a parse failure. Attrition is reported separately for the two distinct causes---molecules for which a notation could not be generated or retrieved, and molecules for which no valid numeric response was obtained---in Table~\ref{tbl:notation} and Section~3.1.

\textbf{Validation that the model performs the task.} A model that emits a near-constant value irrespective of input would exhibit a sign-flip rate determined by the sign of that constant rather than by any property of the notation, and would appear spuriously notation-robust. I therefore verify, per notation, the modal fraction (the share of predictions taking the single most frequent value) and the correlation between predictions and measured $\logS$. For Qwen3.6:35b-a3b the modal fractions are 19.9\% (SMILES), 13.0\% (IUPAC), 20.1\% (InChI) and 23.7\% (SELFIES), with Pearson $r=0.726$, $0.774$, $0.753$ and $0.617$ and Spearman $\rho=0.776$, $0.831$, $0.829$ and $0.662$ respectively (all $p<10^{-100}$). The model is therefore genuinely predicting solubility under every notation, and the cross-notation differences I report reflect notation rather than degenerate output. I note that a low count of \textit{distinct} values is not evidence of degeneracy: the model answers on a coarse one-decimal grid and yields only 2.4--7.1\% unique values across $\sim$1000 molecules while retaining strong rank correlation with ground truth.

\textbf{Screening of additional models.} I screened four further open models under identical prompt and decoding settings, applying the validity criteria above: nous-hermes2:34b (Llama family, 34\,B), qwen2.5:7b, and 4-bit and 2-bit post-training quantisations of the Qwen3.6 architecture. All four failed. nous-hermes2:34b returned a single identical value for all 181 SELFIES prompts and the same value for 88.8\% of SMILES prompts ($r=0.20$); qwen2.5:7b gave $r=0.055$ on SMILES; the 2-bit quantisation gave $r=-0.177$ (Spearman $\rho=0.014$), and the 4-bit variant produced parseable answers for only 4 of 40 molecules. I report these outcomes because they bear directly on reproducibility: aggressive quantisation of this architecture removes the chemical capability required to study notation robustness at all, and a model that emits a different constant per notation will appear notation-sensitive for reasons unrelated to notation. Studying this phenomenon requires a model that demonstrably performs the underlying task, and among the open models available to me only the one reported here did.

\textbf{Model selection rationale.} Qwen3.6:35b-a3b was selected for three reasons. First, its mixture-of-experts architecture activates only $\sim$3\,B of 35\,B parameters per token, enabling full local deployment on a single workstation without quantisation artefacts that could introduce additional notation-dependent noise. Second, it represents the Pareto frontier of open-weight models available at the time of the study: the 35B parameter scale places it in the same tier as GPT-3.5-class models evaluated in prior notation studies,\cite{yan2025} ensuring comparability. Third, its chain-of-thought reasoning can be deterministically disabled (\texttt{think=False}), eliminating stochastic intermediate tokens that would confound the temperature\,=\,0 reproducibility guarantee. A general-purpose rather than chemistry-fine-tuned model was chosen deliberately: my goal is to probe whether general chemical knowledge in large LLMs is notation-invariant, not to benchmark the best achievable accuracy. Chemistry-specific fine-tuning on SMILES could mask notation sensitivity by biasing predictions toward one notation's statistical patterns.

\subsubsection{Metrics.~~} The \textit{spread} for each molecule is defined as $\max(\text{predictions}) - \min(\text{predictions})$ across all notations with valid numerical responses. A molecule is \textit{inconsistent} if spread\,$>0.5$\,$\logS$. A \textit{sign flip} occurs when a molecule with true $\logS < -1$ (insoluble) receives a predicted $\logS > 0$ (highly soluble) under a given notation. I computed 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals ($n_\text{boot}=2000$, reported to 2 decimal places) for all aggregate statistics.

\subsection{Statistical testing}

Differences in four-notation spread across structural classes were assessed with the Kruskal--Wallis $H$-test. Pairwise comparisons used one-tailed Mann--Whitney $U$-tests. The sign-flip advantage of SELFIES over SMILES was tested by a two-sample Fisher exact test ($H_0$: SELFIES sign-flip rate $\geq$ SMILES sign-flip rate). All tests were computed using \texttt{scipy.stats} (v1.16.2).

\subsection{Mechanistic analysis}

\subsubsection{Model and prediction head.~~} I used ChemBERTa-2 (seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1),\cite{ahmad2022} a 6-layer RoBERTa-based SMILES encoder (86\,M parameters, 768-dimensional hidden states) pre-trained on 77\,M ZINC SMILES\cite{irwin2012} via masked language modelling. Although SMILES-only during training, it provides a controlled probe for notation-specific encoding: any representational dependence on notation surface features is attributable to the training distribution.

To make mechanistic interventions causally interpretable---converting hidden-state changes into observable output changes---I attached a linear ridge regression head ($\alpha=1.0$) trained on CLS token embeddings from ChemBERTa-2's final layer for all 1128 ESOL molecules. Five-fold cross-validation yielded MAE\,=\,0.783\,$\logS$, confirming a meaningful prediction signal.

\subsubsection{SMILES augmentation pairs.~~} For all 1128 ESOL molecules, I generated an augmented SMILES \textit{via} SELFIES-based augmentation: SMILES\,$\to$\,SELFIES encoding\,$\to$\,one-position token rotation\,$\to$\,SELFIES decoding\,$\to$\,augmented SMILES. Since SELFIES tokens correspond to atomic sub-structures rather than arbitrary characters, a one-position rotation is guaranteed by the SELFIES grammar to produce a valid, chemically meaningful string. Chemical equivalence of all retained pairs was confirmed by comparing canonical forms obtained \textit{via} SELFIES round-trip; all pairs encoded the same molecular graph. 1051 of 1128 augmentations (93.2\%) yielded valid, distinct SMILES pairs, with 77 augmentation failures excluded.

\subsubsection{Representation-level hidden-state replacement.~~} For each pair (SMILES$_A$, SMILES$_B$), I extract CLS hidden states at all seven positions (embedding layer\,$+$\,six transformer layers). I then replace the CLS hidden state of the SMILES$_B$ forward pass at layer $k$ with the corresponding state from SMILES$_A$ using a registered PyTorch \texttt{forward\_hook}, and measure the resulting $\logS$ prediction from the Ridge head. The improvement at layer $k$ is:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta(k) = \bigl|\hat{y}_A - \hat{y}_B\bigr| - \bigl|\hat{y}_A - \hat{y}_{B}^{(k)}\bigr|
  \label{eq:improvement}
\end{equation}

A positive $\Delta(k)$ indicates that substituting the layer-$k$ CLS state narrows the prediction gap (notation divergence decreases); $\Delta(k)=0$ indicates no effect. Here $\hat{y}_A$ and $\hat{y}_B$ are the unmodified predictions for SMILES$_A$ and SMILES$_B$, and $\hat{y}_{B}^{(k)}$ is the prediction after replacing the layer-$k$ CLS state of SMILES$_B$ with that of SMILES$_A$. I report the layer with maximum $\Delta(k)$ as the most effective intervention layer. I use the conservative term \textit{representation-level hidden-state replacement} throughout, as I do not perform formal causal mediation analysis. 95\% bootstrap CIs ($n_\text{boot}=2000$) are reported for all aggregate metrics.

\subsubsection{Consistency--accuracy orthogonality.~~} I computed Spearman's $\rho$ between the per-pair prediction gap $|\hat{y}_A - \hat{y}_B|$ (inconsistency proxy) and the absolute prediction error $|\hat{y}_A - y^\ast|$ (inaccuracy proxy, where $y^\ast$ is the true $\logS$), testing whether they are statistically independent.

\subsection{The SELFIES sign-flip advantage is broad but not universal across structural classes}
\label{sec:selfies}

SELFIES achieves the lowest overall sign-flip rate (Table~\ref{tbl:notation}), and this advantage holds within most---but, importantly, not all---structural classes (Table~\ref{tbl:class}). For aromatic compounds SELFIES produces just 1 sign-flip error in 267 valid predictions (0.4\%) compared with 12/223 for SMILES (5.4\%); heterocyclic compounds show zero SELFIES sign-flip errors (0/378) versus 15/388 for SMILES (3.9\%); and carboxylic/ester compounds likewise show none (0/51) against 4/50 for SMILES (8.0\%).

Two classes run counter to this pattern. For alcohol/phenol compounds InChI is the safest notation (0/166; 0.0\%) while SELFIES incurs 7 sign-flips in 153 valid predictions (4.6\%). More strikingly, for genuinely halogenated compounds SMILES is the safest notation (6/56; 10.7\%) and SELFIES is worse (8/51; 15.7\%). I note that this reverses the conclusion reported in an earlier version of this work, in which a defect in my structural-class assignment---identified in review and described in Section~2.1.1---placed 264 non-halogenated molecules in the halogenated class and thereby masked the effect.

The corrected classification also reveals that halogenated compounds are markedly the most sign-flip-prone class overall, at every notation, and that IUPAC is catastrophically unreliable for them (21/58; 36.2\%, against an overall IUPAC rate of 7.3\%). Halogen atoms are represented compactly and unambiguously in SMILES but require substituent prefixes and locants in systematic nomenclature, and I hypothesise that this is where the model's grasp of halogen identity is weakest; the hypothesis remains to be tested directly. IUPAC is the most sign-flip-prone notation in the three largest classes (heterocyclic, aromatic, halogenated) but not universally so: SMILES is worst for carboxylic/ester and alcohol/phenol compounds, and InChI for aliphatics.

I continue to hypothesise that SELFIES' validity-by-construction property prevents the model from processing syntactically impossible token sequences, reducing catastrophic failures in aggregate; the two exceptions above indicate that this protection is not uniform across chemistry, and that notation choice is best made with the relevant structural class in mind.

Ring-free carboxylic compounds are significantly more inconsistent than ring-bearing ones ($p=0.002$). The ring system therefore dominates the carbonyl group in determining cross-notation behaviour, and the carboxylic/ester result is most precisely stated as a property of ring-free carbonyl compounds. \subsection{The effect is robust to metric, threshold, and task formulation}
\label{sec:robust}

Three choices in the analysis above could in principle drive the result: the 0.5\,$\logS$ inconsistency threshold, the use of the range rather than a variance-based statistic, and the framing of the task as regression. None of them does.

\textbf{Threshold.} The inconsistency rate declines smoothly and remains substantial across the full plausible range of thresholds: 94.5\% at 0.1\,$\logS$, 93.6\% at 0.25, 88.0\% at 0.5 (as reported above), 80.9\% at 0.75, 61.0\% at 1.0, 46.9\% at 1.5 and 26.3\% at 2.0. Even at a criterion of 1\,$\logS$---an order of magnitude in molar solubility---a majority of molecules receive materially different predictions depending only on how they are written.

\textbf{Metric.} Computing the standard deviation across all four notation-specific predictions rather than the range gives a mean of 0.830\,$\logS$ (median 0.700) and ranks molecules almost identically to the range (Spearman $\rho=0.987$, $p<10^{-300}$, $n=790$). No conclusion depends on the choice. I note one consequence I report against my own interest: under the standard deviation on the matched complete-case set, halogenated compounds are marginally more inconsistent than carboxylic/ester compounds (0.\,1.094 \textit{vs.}\ 1.001), so the identification of a single most-inconsistent class is sensitive to metric and subset, and I state the carboxylic/ester result as holding for the full sample under the range metric.

\textbf{Task formulation.} Large language models are known to be imprecise at numerical regression, raising the question of whether the effect is an artefact of that formulation. It is not. Discretising the same predictions into solubility bands and asking only whether the four notations agree on the \textit{band}, 71.8\% of molecules disagree under a four-band scheme, 45.9\% under a three-band scheme, and 27.8\% under a binary soluble/insoluble split at $\logS=-2$. Band-level accuracy under the binary formulation is 82--90\% across notations, so the model performs the classification competently; the disagreement is not a symptom of numerical imprecision. More than a quarter of molecules receive a different qualitative solubility call depending only on notation.

\subsection{Notation sensitivity is specific to string-based models}
\label{sec:baseline}

A natural question is whether cross-notation inconsistency reflects something particular to language models or is a general property of molecular machine learning. I tested this directly with a non-sequence baseline: a RandomForest regressor trained on eight standard RDKit molecular descriptors over the same 1072 ESOL molecules (5-fold CV MAE 0.237\,$\logS$). Each molecule was then presented to it through four different string routes---canonical SMILES, a randomised non-canonical SMILES, an InChI round-trip, and a SELFIES round-trip.

The descriptor model is effectively notation-invariant. Its mean prediction spread across the four string forms is 0.105\,$\logS$, against 1.686\,$\logS$ for Qwen3.6 across four notations, and only 9.8\% of molecules exceed the 0.5\,$\logS$ threshold against 88.0\%. Moreover its residual variation is not notation sensitivity at all: for 79.2\% of molecules every string route yields a bit-identical Morgan fingerprint and therefore an identical prediction, and the remainder are cases where the InChI or SELFIES round-trip altered the molecular graph through tautomer normalisation or loss of stereochemistry---a property of the round-trip, not of the model.

Cross-notation inconsistency is therefore a property of models that consume molecular \textit{strings}. A descriptor- or fingerprint-based model cannot exhibit it, because its representation collapses all string forms of a molecule to the same input vector. I note also that the descriptor baseline is considerably more accurate than any language model evaluated here (0.237 against a best notation-specific MAE of 0.887\,$\logS$); my argument concerns consistency rather than accuracy, but the comparison is important context for practitioners.

\subsection{The effect replicates on a second property}
\label{sec:freesolv}

To test whether these findings are specific to aqueous solubility, I repeated the behavioural protocol on FreeSolv\cite{mobley2014} (hydration free energy, kcal\,mol$^{-1}$) using the three notations that can be generated locally (SMILES, InChI, SELFIES). The model performs this task genuinely in all three (Pearson $r=0.646$, $0.599$, $0.460$).

The effect replicates closely. On FreeSolv 81.9\% of molecules exceed a 0.5-unit three-notation spread, against 83.6\% for the same three notations on ESOL; the mean spread is 3.364\,kcal\,mol$^{-1}$ and 1.404\,$\logS$ respectively, which normalised by the range of each target variable is 14.3\% and 13.0\%. A different property, in different units, over different molecules, yields cross-notation inconsistency of near-identical relative magnitude.

Importantly, the notation \textit{ranking} does not transfer. InChI achieves the lowest error on ESOL but the highest of the three on FreeSolv (MAE 3.054 \textit{vs.}\ 2.596 for SMILES). I therefore present the existence and magnitude of notation sensitivity as generalising, while treating any specific notation recommendation as task-dependent (Section~\ref{sec:recommend}).

I also attempted the two classification datasets the analysis would ideally include (BBBP and BACE). On both, Qwen3.6 failed to exceed the majority-class baseline in any notation, and its answers were strongly and degenerately notation-biased---on BACE it answered positive for 88.7\% of SMILES prompts but 8.0\% of SELFIES prompts, at 50.0\% accuracy against a 60.0\% majority class. A naive reading of those runs would report 90.0\% cross-notation label disagreement, which I do not, because a model that cannot perform a task cannot be used to measure notation robustness on it. Whether the effect extends to molecular classification therefore remains open, and requires a model demonstrably capable of the task.

\subsection{Mechanistic analysis: progressive representational divergence with encoder depth}

Augmented SMILES pairs representing identical molecules differ substantially in their predicted $\logS$: the mean prediction gap is 0.873\,$\logS$ [95\% CI: 0.825,\,0.922; median 0.658\,$\logS$] across all 1051 pairs. The mean final-layer cosine similarity between pairs is 0.660 [95\% CI: 0.644,\,0.676], substantially below 1.0, confirming that chemically equivalent SMILES occupy meaningfully different positions in the learned embedding space.

Tracing CLS cosine similarity across all seven hidden-state positions shows that this separation accumulates monotonically with depth rather than arising at any single layer (Fig.~\ref{fig:layers}). Mean similarity falls from 0.9868 at $L_1$ through 0.9228, 0.8584, 0.8040 and 0.7548 to 0.6597 at the final layer, with the largest single decrements between $L_1$ and $L_3$ and at the final layer. Taking a similarity of 0.85 as a divergence threshold, 663 of 1051 pairs cross it, and onset is concentrated at $L_2$ (186 pairs) and $L_3$ (303 pairs). The embedding position is identical for every molecule in the dataset (cosine 1.0000, s.d.\ 0.0000) because the pre-attention CLS state is a function of token and position embeddings alone; it therefore carries no molecular information and is excluded from all subsequent analysis.

Representational divergence predicts the behavioural quantity. Cosine similarity correlates negatively with the prediction gap at every layer, strengthening with depth to Spearman $\rho\,=\,-0.371$ ($p\,=\,1.4\!\times\!10^{-35}$, $n=1051$) at the final layer, with monotone dose-response across similarity quintiles: mean gap rises from 0.324\,$\logS$ among the most-similar fifth of pairs to 1.129\,$\logS$ among the least-similar. Molecules whose two notations are encoded more similarly receive more consistent predictions.

Table~\ref{tbl:phase2} breaks down these results by structural class. Aromatic compounds show both the lowest mean cosine similarity (0.417) and the largest mean gap (1.120\,$\logS$), with heterocyclic compounds close behind (0.573; 1.029\,$\logS$). Halogenated compounds are the most stable on both measures (0.953; 0.429\,$\logS$). I offer the interpretation---as a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism---that this ordering reflects where two valid strings for identical chemistry differ most: aromatic ring systems admit both kekulized and lowercase-aromatic forms as well as alternative ring-closure indexing, whereas a halogen atom is represented by a single unambiguous token in either string.

Single-layer hidden-state replacement narrows this divergence only marginally. Reporting the full per-layer distribution before any selection, mean improvement is $-0.006$, $-0.012$, $+0.089$, $+0.038$ and $+0.096$\,$\logS$ at $L_1$ through $L_5$ respectively, with 49.1\%, 50.0\%, 44.1\%, 45.7\% and 42.9\% of pairs degraded at each layer. One-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against zero, Bonferroni-corrected for five comparisons ($\alpha=0.01$), find a significant positive effect at $L_3$ ($p=6.1\!\times\!10^{-6}$) and $L_5$ ($p=4.7\!\times\!10^{-5}$), a marginal effect at $L_4$ ($p=8.97\!\times\!10^{-3}$), and no effect at $L_1$ or $L_2$. The effect at the responsive layers is real but small: the largest mean improvement (0.096\,$\logS$ at $L_5$) recovers approximately 11\% of the mean prediction gap and lies well below the prediction head's own cross-validated MAE of 0.783\,$\logS$, and even at this layer 42.9\% of individual pairs are made worse. Middle-to-late layers therefore carry some of the information responsible for divergence, but no single-layer intervention approaches reconciling the two representations.

I note that the final position cannot serve as an intervention site at all: because the prediction head reads the final-layer CLS state, replacing it substitutes the source molecule's prediction wholesale, and the resulting ``improvement'' equals the unpatched gap to within $5\!\times\!10^{-5}$ for all 1051 pairs. Both the embedding and final positions are therefore excluded, and only $L_1$--$L_5$ are analysed as interventions.

\subsubsection{Controls.~~} To establish that these effects are not artifacts of the intervention procedure, I ran four controls over all 1051 pairs at each of $L_1$--$L_5$. Replacing a molecule's CLS state with its own (canonical control) produces a deviation of exactly zero at every layer, for every pair, confirming that the hook mechanism, CLS substitution as such, and the Ridge head introduce no effect of their own. Replacing with an unrelated molecule's state is by contrast strongly damaging, and increasingly so with depth ($-0.076$, $-0.223$, $-0.458$, $-0.688$, $-0.826$\,$\logS$ at $L_1$--$L_5$). The reversed-direction control is approximately neutral ($+0.008$ to $-0.092$\,$\logS$).

The contrast between true-partner and random-molecule replacement is the informative quantity. True-partner substitution outperforms random-molecule substitution at every layer, and the margin grows monotonically with depth, from $+0.070\,\logS$ at $L_1$ to $+0.922\,\logS$ at $L_5$ (Wilcoxon $p$ from $3.2\!\times\!10^{-10}$ to $1.1\!\times\!10^{-80}$; Spearman $\rho=1.000$ for margin against depth; true exceeds random in 58.7\% of pairs at $L_1$ rising to 75.5\% at $L_5$). Diverged representations therefore remain strongly molecule-specific: the encoder retains information identifying which molecule it is reading, and increasingly so through depth, even as the two notations' representations separate. Taken with the preceding result---that substituting this shared information recovers only a small fraction of the prediction gap---this locates cross-notation inconsistency as a failure to converge on a common encoding rather than a loss of chemical identity. I note that this control excludes hook-mechanism and head artifacts but does not establish that cross-molecule substitution remains on the model's activation manifold; I return to this limitation in Section~4.

\subsection{Consistency and accuracy are orthogonal}

I observe Spearman $\rho = -0.059$ ($p=0.055$; $n=1051$) between per-pair cross-notation gap and absolute prediction error---no significant correlation. At this sample size, a Spearman test has $>80\%$ power to detect $|\rho|\geq 0.086$; the estimated $\rho=-0.059$ falls well below this detectable threshold, indicating that any true correlation is negligibly small even if it exists. The slightly negative $\rho$ is consistent with inconsistent pairs spanning wider prediction ranges, occasionally landing closer to the true value by chance. This result replicates the key finding of Yan \textit{et al.}\cite{yan2025}---who demonstrated the same orthogonality at LLM output level---and, to my knowledge, is the first demonstration at the level of encoder CLS representations ($n=1051$ pairs): a SMILES encoder that produces similar embeddings for augmented pairs does not systematically yield more accurate $\logS$ predictions. Conversely, high SMILES accuracy cannot be taken as evidence that the encoder has internalised a notation-robust molecular concept.

This orthogonality has a direct and important practical implication: \textbf{fine-tuning on accuracy metrics alone will not solve notation inconsistency.} Because inconsistency and inaccuracy are statistically independent ($\rho=-0.059$, $p=0.055$), optimising a model's loss on SMILES-based accuracy benchmarks applies no gradient pressure to reduce cross-notation divergence. A model could achieve state-of-the-art SMILES MAE while still producing predictions that differ by $>2\,\logS$ when the same molecule is presented in InChI or SELFIES format. Resolving this requires either dedicated consistency training objectives---such as consistency regularisation across notation pairs---or notation-invariant representations enforced at the architecture level. Standard accuracy benchmarks, which almost exclusively use SMILES,\cite{ganeeva2025} are therefore insufficient to characterise model understanding, and dedicated cross-notation consistency benchmarks are a necessary complement.

\subsection{Practical recommendations}
\label{sec:recommend}

My findings support four actionable recommendations for practitioners using LLMs for molecular property prediction.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Choose notation based on the error type you wish to minimise---and on the chemistry at hand.} SELFIES achieves the lowest overall sign-flip rate (1.7\%; a rate 3.2$\times$ lower than SMILES; OR\,=\,3.34 for SMILES relative to SELFIES, $p\,=\,6.2\!\times\!10^{-6}$) and is the safest choice when catastrophic solubility-sign reversals are the primary concern---particularly for heterocyclic (0/378 SELFIES sign-flips), carboxylic/ester (0/51) and aromatic (1/267) compounds. This advantage is not universal, however: for alcohol/phenol compounds InChI is safer (0/166 \textit{vs.}\ 7/153), and for halogenated compounds SMILES is safer than SELFIES (10.7\% \textit{vs.}\ 15.7\%). InChI achieves the lowest MAE (0.887\,$\logS$; 21.4\% lower than SMILES MAE of 1.127\,$\logS$) and is preferable when minimising average prediction error across the full dynamic range is the priority. IUPAC should be used with particular caution for halogenated compounds, where its sign-flip rate reaches 36.2\% against an overall rate of 7.3\%.
\item \textbf{Report the input notation explicitly} in LLM chemistry methods sections. Notation is a hidden confound: switching from SMILES to IUPAC can shift predicted $\logS$ by up to 6.00 units for individual molecules (Table~\ref{tbl:top10}) and by a mean of $\sim$2.15\,$\logS$ for carboxylic/ester compounds. Without notation disclosure, published LLM solubility benchmarks are not directly comparable.
\item \textbf{Do not use single-notation accuracy as a proxy for molecular understanding.} Because consistency and accuracy are orthogonal (Spearman $\rho=-0.059$, $p=0.055$, $n=1051$), high SMILES accuracy provides no guarantee of generalisation to other representations. Fine-tuning on accuracy metrics alone will not close the cross-notation consistency gap.
\item \textbf{Target representational convergence, not output agreement.} My mechanistic results show that divergence between notations accumulates progressively with encoder depth and is already substantial by $L_2$--$L_3$, and that no single-layer intervention reverses it. Because diverged representations nonetheless retain molecule-specific information, the architecturally motivated target for notation-robust pre-training is a consistency objective applied across intermediate layers---encouraging convergence as representations are built---rather than a penalty on output disagreement alone, which my orthogonality result indicates accuracy-driven training will not supply.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Limitations}

I test a single general-purpose LLM; results may differ for chemistry-fine-tuned models. Phase\,2 uses within-SMILES augmented pairs rather than true cross-notation pairs, because ChemBERTa-2 is a SMILES-only encoder; a full cross-notation mechanistic study would require a multi-notation foundation model such as a jointly trained SMILES/IUPAC encoder. Phase\,1 covers 1072 of 1128 ESOL molecules (94.9\%); 56 molecules were excluded due to PubChem API lookup failures. Phase\,2 covers 1051 valid augmented-SMILES pairs, providing high statistical power throughout.

\section*{Conclusions}

I have demonstrated that notation inconsistency in LLM-based molecular property prediction is (i) pervasive---affecting 88.0\% of 1072 ESOL molecules with a mean four-notation spread of 1.69\,$\logS$ (median 1.30\,$\logS$), occurring even when the model is explicitly informed of the notation type; (ii) structurally moderated---carboxylic/ester compounds being most severely affected (mean spread 2.15\,$\logS$; Kruskal--Wallis $p=1.2\!\times\!10^{-7}$); (iii) notation-specifically modulated: SELFIES achieves the lowest overall sign-flip rate (1.7\% \textit{vs.}\ 5.4\% for SMILES; OR\,=\,3.34, $p=6.2\!\times\!10^{-6}$) and the lowest rate in most structural classes, though not in alcohol/phenol or halogenated compounds, while InChI achieves the lowest MAE (0.887\,$\logS$), revealing that error type, notation choice and structural class are jointly important; and (iv)---crucially, going beyond prior purely behavioural work\cite{yan2025,ganeeva2025}---mechanistically traceable to a progressive failure of representational convergence ($n=1051$ augmented-SMILES pairs): CLS representations of chemically identical strings separate monotonically with encoder depth, with onset at $L_2$--$L_3$ and final-layer cosine similarity of 0.660, and the degree of separation predicts the magnitude of prediction disagreement (Spearman $\rho=-0.371$; $p=1.4\!\times\!10^{-35}$). Controls show that diverged representations remain molecule-specific---substitution by an unrelated molecule is markedly more damaging than substitution by the true notation partner at every layer---yet no single-layer replacement closes the prediction gap. Notation divergence in a chemistry encoder is therefore better characterised as a failure to converge on a shared encoding than as a loss of chemical identity, which locates the problem in how representations are built across depth rather than at any single position. The orthogonality of inconsistency and inaccuracy (Spearman $\rho=-0.059$; $p=0.055$; $n=1051$) confirms that fine-tuning on accuracy metrics alone cannot resolve the cross-notation consistency problem. I advocate for standardised notation reporting in LLM chemistry benchmarks, and recommend selecting input notation based on the specific error type to minimise: SELFIES for sign-flip safety, InChI for average accuracy.
